# Taste

A day is one line with three words hidden. The player rebuilds them by typing guesses
and reading how close each one lands. Everything here serves one moment: **a word
lands, and the line changes.** The player could reason their way to it, and it still
surprises: inevitable once found, never obvious before. When a word lands and nothing
changes — "of course it was that" — the day is dead, however well it follows the rules.

A good day gives every hidden word a **path**: a player who reads the visible line
carefully can reason toward the word, and when it lands the click and the smile come
together. The failure to design against is the **lottery word**: one member of a wide kind
that the line does not narrow (« judo » among all sports, « septembre » among all months).
The player can only try the members one by one, and the ranks cannot tell siblings apart.
Lines are not all built the same way: the seven kinds below give paths reliably, and each
one says where the path runs and where the word lands.

Taste is judged by comparison, not by checklist. Put candidates side by side and choose
the one you would be proudest to publish; when none is good, publish nothing. The
examples are the measure — real days the game shipped, and a few candidates the curator
proposed — and the words around them only point at what they share.

## The voice

The references are **Houellebecq, Murakami and Kundera**. What they share — and what the
game looks for — is not a mood but a way of seeing modern life from slightly too far:
work, love, bodies, cities, the market reaching into everything. And most of the time,
it is a joke.

- **Funny first.** Dry, absurd, cruel-precise. Very often the grand brought down to the
  petty: years of practice and natural gifts… to breed pigeons; all human knowledge…
  against a gnat; the last man on earth… checking his watch. The sad lines that work
  have a joke in them too.
- **Exact words on intimate things.** The vocabulary of science, sociology, statistics or
  administration laid on love, loneliness or death; the gap between the cold word and
  the warm subject is the joke and the sadness at once. « mammifères intelligents, qui
  auraient pu s'aimer ».
- **The banal detail carries the big thing.** A neon sign for a brand of beer gives a
  face the majesty of a police demon; the end of the world comes down to a watch in a
  pocket. The contemporary, concrete, almost administrative detail — the one a lyrical
  writer would cut — is where the line happens.
- **Lucid, then moved anyway.** It knows how common everything is, says so, and is still
  touched. Tenderness is proven by facts, never declared with adjectives; contempt for
  human nature and love for people sit in the same sentence.
- **Self-mockery with precision.** The speaker is the first target, dryly, without
  self-pity.
- **The turn comes last and short.** The line builds a small system; its last words
  knock it over.
- **Two registers in one breath.** The learned and the spoken side by side — a salon
  wit's phrasing about a fat man, a rapper's pun on the motherland.

What it never is: lyrical, solemn, a lesson, a verdict on the reader — and never
**reactionary**: no line that mocks a group of people or longs for the old order, and no
line from a passage that does, since the page shows it (Muray on the gay activists of the
Pacs: a light joke, a sneering page). A mood alone is not the voice: a line that is only
sad, or a little off in tone, is the weaker kind of day.
And the worst kind is **slop** — a line chosen because nothing is wrong with it: a piece
of plot told in plain words, a stock image, three ordinary words. This is the game's voice
and it does not move.

## The line

A line is alive when it DOES something: it turns, it bites, it shows. Measured on 58
days of share rate (unique shares / unique solves, 2026-07-08 → 09-05; mean 0.63, noise
about ±0.15 on one day):

| Feature of the line | Share rate with | without |
| --- | --- | --- |
| An unexpected source (Darwin, Marx, a documentary, a president, the *Hagakure*) | **0.77** | 0.61 |
| Wit — irony, a dry joke, a cruel-precise comparison | **0.70** | 0.59 |
| A turn — the line lands on a reversal or a punch | **0.69** | 0.59 |
| An image carrying a thought | 0.68 | — |
| An image and nothing else (pure description) | **0.55** | 0.65 |
| Despair with no wit and no tenderness | **0.54** | 0.65 |
| Abstract nouns without wit (*néant, âme, ineffable, réalité*) | **0.51** | 0.65 |
| A verdict on the reader ("tu t'accoutumes à la médiocrité") | **0.25** | 0.64 |

Not every good day is a joke: an image carrying a thought plays well too (0.68 above) —
a city at nightfall, a mood with a thought in it. What does not matter: the kind (book,
music, film), the length. Half of the best days
come from outside the novel — a naturalist, an entomologist, an anthropologist, a
gastronome, rap.

Alive (the hidden words in brackets), the favourites first:
- « une michetonneuse, un mec [fauché], ça va pas ensemble, top model et clic-clac, les
  [rolex], les flik flak, l'alcool dans l'sang et les routes en [zigzag] » (Alpha Wann) —
  love can't cross the money gap, said only in mismatched pairs; the last pair matches
  too well, and it kills (kind 1, *Pairs and molds*).
- « j'aime tes "je t'aime" sur les [post-it], tes vrais et tes faux [cils], les mauvais
  et les bons [signes] » (NeS) — loving someone whole, the fake and the bad included,
  folded into a « je t'aime » (kind 1).
- « ici, c'est trop [bruyant], le capharnaüm, on différencie de moins en moins le
  [cafard] de l'[homme] » (JeanJass) — the cruelty lands on the last word.
- « à la [lueur] rouge, vacillante, d'une réclame au néon pour une marque de [bière], le
  visage de Horiki me semblait avoir pris la majesté d'un [démon] policier » (Dazai) — a
  grotesque image built from a beer ad.
- « peu de personnes s'imaginent combien il faut de capacités [naturelles], combien il
  faut d'années de pratique pour faire un bon [éleveur] de [pigeons] » (Darwin) — a
  solemn build-up, and pigeons.
- « j'dors pas, j'prends jamais d'[repos] j'vais t'aimer et [mourir] comme un putain de
  [robot] » (Rounhaa) — a love line that ends on a machine.
- « ce qui [séduisait] surtout certaines, c'est qu'il portait, possiblement, dans l'âme,
  un genre de [secret] fatal ; d'autres aimaient positivement qu'il fût un [assassin] »
  (Dostoïevski) — irony that tightens to the last word.
- « Mirabeau disait d'un homme excessivement [gros], que Dieu ne l'avait créé que pour
  [montrer] jusqu'à quel point la [peau] humaine pouvait s'étendre sans rompre »
  (Brillat-Savarin) — a cruel joke told with a physiologist's precision.
- « le [dernier] homme, au moment de quitter cette terre froide et [dévastée], aura dans
  sa [poche] une montre, pour savoir l'heure exacte de sa mort » (Machado de Assis) —
  the end of the world, and a watch.
- « un grand silence s'est installé dans le [bureau]. je me [retourne], je lance à la
  cantonade, d'une voix forte : "j'ai rendez-vous avec un [psychiatre] !" et je sors »
  (Houellebecq) — a deadpan exit.

Dead — slop, from the curator's September. Slop is PLOT, not thought: a character's
situation or a scene's mechanics, told in plain words, that means nothing beyond its
story.
- « J'ai appris à [lâcher] d'emblée que mon [alcoolique] de mari est mort depuis peu, me
  laissant moi et mes quatre [gosses]. » (Lucia Berlin, 2026-09-13) — a situation,
  reported: widowed, four children. Information, not a line.
- « J'avais tellement besoin d'une étreinte [amicale] que j'ai failli me pendre.
  Heureusement Gros-Câlin avait froid, j'avais astucieusement fermé le [chauffage] exprès
  pour ça et il est venu m'[envelopper], en ronronnant de plaisir. » (Gary, 2026-09-11) —
  an anecdote whose joke needs the book (Gros-Câlin is a python); read alone, it is a
  muddle.
- « La pièce en elle-même était [nulle]. Mais Charlotte sur scène, elle vous [lisait]
  l'annuaire téléphonique et vous tombiez à la renverse tellement c'était [beau]. »
  (Dicker, 2026-09-24) — an opinion reported; reading the phone book is a stock phrase.

Dull — the device works, and nobody is touched:
- « Arrêt cardiaque. Pedro s'appelait Pierre, mais il prenait tant de cocaïne qu'il avait
  hérité d'un prénom sud-américain. » (Despentes, 2026-09-10) — a gag about a stranger's
  nickname.
- « L'une après l'autre, les agences refusent de prendre mes coordonnées. On me traite
  avec une douceur d'infirmière dans un service de soins palliatifs, mais fermement. »
  (Aubenas, 2026-09-17) — a well-made comparison about job agencies.

A line with a thought in it — even a solemn one, even an abstract one — is never slop; at
worst it is heavy.

Ask: would you read it aloud to someone, and would they laugh, wince or stop?

## The book

The book sets the ceiling. A writer whose sentences turn gives a day on almost any page;
plain narrative prose — a thriller's, a chronicle's — reports what happens and leaves
nothing to rebuild. When a book's best lines are all dead, the answer is another book.

Never a **reactionary book**. The author is not the test, the book and the line are:
Houellebecq is a reference, his *Soumission* is out, and a reactionary line is out
whoever wrote it.

## Words used here

- **Main word:** the word the line is about: the punch its kind names or, in a line with
  no kind, the word the line lands on. The two other hidden words are chosen against it.
- **Path:** the line leads a careful reader to the meaning, and the hidden word is among
  the first few words they would type for that meaning. A short walk through synonyms that
  ends on the common word counts as a path. A synonym that nothing in the line picks out
  does not.
- **Lottery:** one member of a wide kind that the line does not narrow. Also one word
  among equal synonyms, with nothing to pick it.
- **No clue:** the line does not even give the kind, or it points somewhere else.
- **Unsaid:** the meaning has a path, but players say a different word for it.
- **Handed:** the line writes the word in: most readers put it straight into the blank.
  When the few words right around the hole write it in on their own (« le droit de ___ »
  is « vote »), its neighbours hand it over, and that word is never hidden (*Per trio*).
- **Locked pair:** two holes that can each be reached only through the other.

## The order of checks

1. **The line is alive.** Apply *The voice*, *The line* and *The book* first: would you
   read it aloud, and would the listener laugh, wince or stop? A kind guarantees paths,
   not life. Aubenas (09-17), Botchan (10-06 en), Vonnegut (10-07 en) and Ernaux (08-21)
   were each built correctly and were dull.
2. **It has a construction:** one of the seven kinds, or a line strong enough to go
   without one (see *Lines that fit no kind*).
3. **Every hole has a path from that construction**, and the punch above all.
4. **Each start word.**

## Rules for every kind

Most lines fit some kind loosely, and about one in five fits one cleanly: these rules are
the real filter.

### The line stands alone, word by word

Every noun means something inside the line. A word that needs the book's plot can only be
guessed, not reasoned toward. This filter alone catches « alcoolique », « facho »,
« dressmaker », « envelopper », « héros » and « masse ». Such a word is never hidden, and a
line whose main word needs the book has no day in it.

### The blank read

Read the line with the holes empty. If the joke can already be read, the trio is only
hiding the setting around it: Vonnegut's men's room, Despentes's « prénom sud-américain »,
Bukowski's « suicidée ». A small fact left visible is fine when it means nothing until a
hidden word lands (Tocqueville's « tutoyer »).

### The punch has a path

A liked day can carry one weak word beside the punch, but never a weak punch. Seven liked
days hid a lottery word and nine hid a handed one, and in each case that word sat beside a
punch that had a path; on the broken days, the lottery was the punch. No liked day hid a
lottery punch, a no-clue word or a locked pair. The weak word beside the punch may be a
handed one or one players don't say (*Per trio*), never a lottery: a word of a wide kind
whose ladder misses a step is refused on every hole, the main word and the two others
alike (below).

### The narrowing ladder

It applies to any word taken from a wide kind.

1. The slot shapes a kind: « un bon éleveur de ___ » means bred animals.
2. The start's first idea names a smaller family inside that kind: « papillons »
   (creatures people keep), « bourdon » (insects), « opium » (a smoked vice), « cartier »
   (luxury watches), « cookie » (a treat).
3. The hidden word is the first member players name in that family, or the ranks climb to
   it in about three steps (insecte → mouche → moucheron).

If any step is missing, the word is a lottery, and it is refused, as the main word and as
one of the two others. Reza's « septembre » fails at step 2: months have no smaller family,
so no start can save it. « paintball » for « judo » only repeats the kind. NeS's « signes »
(08-11) fails at step 2 too: signs, good or bad, have no smaller family. The sibling test
(swap the word for its nearest sibling: is the joke just as funny?) is a warning only,
because Darwin, Fabre and Fante fail it too, and all three played well.

### No mirror

Never hide the first word players would type as the partner of a visible word. That covers
its opposite (gentil → « méchant »), its synonym, its textbook complement, and the other
half of a set phrase or a compound (« high [jumper] », « the [men]'s room », « arrêt
[cardiaque] »). There is one exception: a hidden word that lands a second meaning (« dame
[nation] », heard as damnation).

### One idea per hole

Never split one idea over neighbouring holes (« [folk] [dancing] », « un [lexique] [rural]
[autrichien] »). A trade and its object are two ideas (« [éleveur] de [pigeons] »).

### No locked pair

« aussi [involontaire] que la [transpiration] » and « entre la [messe] et l'[exécution] »
each hide two words that can only be found through each other.

### A repeated word is one hole with two frames

It helps when each frame alone is wide (« jungle », « serpent »). It hands the word over
when one frame alone is enough to give it.

### Sound confirms the meaning

Rhyme or repeated syllables can lead to a word only when the meaning leads there too, and
at least two visible words must show the sound (« clic-clac », « flik flak » →
« [zigzag] »). « capacity… verged on [rapacity] » has the sound but no step in meaning
from suffering to greed.

### Per trio

At most one unsaid word, and only one worth the search: two in one trio made the worst
days (09-16, 09-22, 09-23, 10-05 fr, finished by a median 40% of the players). Aim for at
most one handed word. The three liked days with two handed words (08-07, 09-09, 09-12)
were saved by the line alone. A word that the few words right around it write in on their
own — « le droit de ___ » is « vote », « cinq semaines de ___ payés » is « congés », found
in the first two guesses — is never that one handed word: it is refused on every hole, the
main word and the two others alike.

## The seven kinds

| # | Kind | Model day | The punch is |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Pairs and molds | « ça va pas ensemble » (08-17 fr), the post-it line (08-11 fr) | one half of a pair, with its partner in plain sight |
| 2 | The build-up and the drop | Darwin's pigeons (08-05 fr) | the small, petty last word |
| 3 | The small fact that proves a big thing | Tocqueville (10-14 fr) | the big meaning behind a small visible fact |
| 4 | The reasoning pushed to its end | Graeber (09-08 fr) | the word where the logic lands |
| 5 | The riddle | « comme un putain de robot » (09-01 fr) | the thing that the visible qualities describe |
| 6 | The cold exact word | « mammifères intelligents » (09-05 fr) | the learned word laid on a warm subject |
| 7 | The bent phrase | « dame nation » (09-06 fr) | the point where a phrase everyone knows bends |

### 1. Pairs and molds: the line teaches its rule

**What.** The line sets things side by side under one rule. Each hidden word is one half of
a pair, or one filling of a mold repeated two or three times, and the rest of that pair or
mold is in plain sight. There are two forms:
- **Pairs under a stated rule:** « ça va pas ensemble », « tu l'appelles…, je
  l'appelle… », « comme la plupart des… ».
- **One mold said two or three times:** « tes vrais et tes faux ___ », « du phosphore
  comme les ___ ».

**Why it plays.** The rule says what kind of word each hole needs, and the visible partner
says which one. What doesn't go with a gold-digger? A guy who's broke. What comes real and
fake? Eyelashes. Each answer is a small deduction that lands a small joke, because the pair
itself is the joke. Each word found makes the rule clearer for the next hole. The best last
pair bends the rule: alcohol and swerving roads *do* go together.

**Spot it.** Ask two questions:
- Quoted alone, does each pair make someone smile? « fauché » with a michetonneuse,
  « rolex » against the flik flak, « cafard » and « homme », « marins » who can't swim:
  yes. Money and moneylenders: no.
- Can the fillings be written as one column of two or three concrete nouns, each next to
  its visible partner, with the last one the oddest?

Cues that find these lines: « pas X mais Y », « alors que », « ce qu'on appelle X… »,
« plus de X que de Y », « la plupart des… comme la plupart des… »; 'not X but Y', 'instead
of', 'whereas', 'respectively'. In songs, look for lists of matched couples (« top model et
clic-clac »).

Cues that mislead: « ni… ni » mostly turns up mirrors and lists of kinds, and a phrase
repeated at the head of each clause mostly turns up refrains that narrow nothing. Four or
more molds can still work when each one carries its own visible partner. A list of bare
nouns does not.

**Holes and punch.**
- Put one hole in each pair or mold, in the half that the visible partner narrows.
- Each mold must pin down a short family. « tes vrais et tes faux ___ » leaves a few body
  parts. « les mauvais et les bons ___ » leaves anything at all: players typed « côtés »,
  far off.
- Put the punch in the pair or mold with the tightest family. That is often the last one,
  the one that bends the rule (« zigzag »), but not always: NeS's last mold, « signes », is
  its lottery, and its best landing is « cils ».
- Let the rule be read before the hardest hole: either make the first pair easy, or leave
  one pair or mold fully visible (Flaubert's « de l'albumine comme les blancs d'œufs »).
- Hide both halves of one pair only in two cases: another pair has already taught the rule
  (Laferrière: « marins… ne savent pas nager » teaches it, then « écrivains » and « lire »
  narrow each other), or grammar or a cliché writes one half in (JeanJass: « le ___ de
  l'[homme] »).
- The relation must be a twist: a mismatch, a confusion, a pun, a counterpart from another
  field. Never a plain opposite, and never a literal one-to-one lookup.

**Start words.**
- The start sits on the hidden word's side of the pair, inside its family, and the visible
  partner does the choosing: « cartier » for « rolex », « affamé » for « fauché »,
  « mille-pattes » for « cafard », « mutins » for « marins », « enveloppes » for
  « post-it », « iris » for « cils », « brindilles » for « allumettes ».
- When the partner is strong, take the start from the far half of the band.
- When both halves are hidden, or the family is wide, the start does the narrowing, on the
  word's direct meaning: « traducteurs » for « écrivains ».
- Never so near that the start finishes the mold for the player (« néons » at 53 for
  « réverbères »). Never a start whose first idea is a side issue: « litigation » for
  « divorce » brought up the courts, not the end of a marriage.

**Examples.**
- **2026-08-17 fr, Alpha Wann (liked; the user's favourite):** « une michetonneuse, un mec
  [fauché], ça va pas ensemble, top model et clic-clac, les [rolex], les flik flak, l'alcool
  dans l'sang et les routes en [zigzag] »
- **2026-08-11 fr, NeS (liked; « signes » is a lottery, *The narrowing ladder*):**
  « j'aime tes "je t'aime" sur les [post-it], tes vrais et tes faux [cils], les
  mauvais et les bons [signes] »
- **2026-10-09 fr, Laferrière (liked):** « la plupart des [écrivains] n'aiment pas [lire],
  comme la plupart des [marins] ne savent pas nager. »

**Broken.**
- **A mirror:** « des gens biens se [reposent] pendant que des enfoirés travaillent dur »
  (09-03 fr); « ni [méchant] ni gentil » (09-27 fr); « au large… sur le [rivage] » (10-01
  fr).
- **A literal rule:** money, authority, reasoning → « [moneylenders], [policemen], and
  college [professors] » (10-06 en). Each hole is a one-step lookup, and the joke only lands
  once all three are filled.
- **A list of kinds:** « aucun [trophée], aucun haut-fait, …, aucun [vaisseau], aucune
  villa » (08-19 fr). Each mold gives a kind, never a member.
- **A refrain that narrows nothing:** « le [jardin] était un [royaume] vert et doré, le
  [jardin] était le monde et la [lumière] ne finissait pas » (08-10 fr).
- **One good pair among lotteries:** « socialiste, mais je deviens [propriétaire] furieux »
  works, but « [bâtons] » and « [cerises] » could be anything thrown and any fruit (09-18
  fr).
- **A hidden half that is one of a wide kind, with a start off to the side:** « the
  [suicide] [jokes]… the ones about [divorce] » (10-10 en).

### 2. The build-up and the drop

**What.** A grand, solemn opening (natural gifts and years of practice, all human
knowledge, a biblical apostrophe, the science of administration) promises something big.
The last word is small, concrete and petty, and the turn comes last and is short.

A close form is the series that climbs one step too far, as in Dostoïevski (09-02 fr):
« possiblement » a fatal « [secret] », then « positivement » an « [assassin] ».

**Why it plays.** The build-up makes a slot whose shape narrows the last word to a kind:
bred animals, a library category, something tiny, a smoked vice. Players guess grand first
and the ranks tell them off. The drop is the reasoning: the smallest, most everyday member
of that kind. When it lands, the whole build-up collapses into a laugh.

**Spot it.** Ask: is the last noun the first member players would name in the kind the
build-up sets up, or one that the start and the ranks can narrow to (the ladder)?
« fiction » for a library category and « cigarettes » for a smoked vice pass. « judo »,
« hockey », « colonel » and « reformation » do not.

The cues: a high register first (« peu de personnes s'imaginent combien », « le savoir
humain », 'thou… leadeth', 'heaven forbid', God, history, the end of the world), then a
turn word just before a short last clause (« mais », « puis », « avant que », 'provided
only that', 'except possibly', 'disappointingly'). Nearly every witty line opens high, so
these cues only find candidates. The last-noun question is what picks one.

**Holes and punch.**
- Hide the petty last word, which is the punch.
- Also hide one or two build-up words that set the stakes and have their own path
  (« naturelles », « archives », « whore »).
- The punch must pass the ladder.
- Never leave the petty end visible while the build-up is hidden. That fails the blank
  read.
- Never pick a punch that its own visible first half writes in (« a high [jumper] »).
- Never hide two adjacent words at the end.
- A list of three whose last item breaks the class may work the same way: keep at least
  one item that sets the class visible, and hide the item that breaks it, which must be the
  first member players would name of its kind. No published day has proven this form yet.

**Start words.**
- The punch's start names the kind the slot shapes, through a neutral member that is not
  petty, so the player still has to make the drop: « papillons » for « pigeons »,
  « bourdon » for « moucheron », « opium » for « cigarettes », « poids » for « place »,
  « cadavre » for « cercueil ».
- Never the answer's next-door neighbour, and never a riddle: « berceau » for « cercueil »
  got no progress at all.
- Build-up words get a medium start from the grand side: « fauconnier », « culturel »,
  « concubine ».

**Examples.**
- **2026-08-05 fr, Darwin (liked):** « peu de personnes s’imaginent combien il faut de
  capacités [naturelles], combien il faut d’années de pratique pour faire un bon [éleveur]
  de [pigeons] »
- **2026-09-07 fr, Fabre (liked; « humain » is written in by « le savoir ___ », *Per
  trio*):** « le savoir [humain] sera rayé des [archives] du monde avant que
  nous ayons le dernier mot d’un [moucheron]. »
- **2026-10-09 en, Parkinson (liked):** « heaven forbid that [students] should cease to
  read books on the science of public or business [administration] — provided only that
  these works are classified as [fiction]. »

**Broken.**
- **A lottery punch:** « je pense me mettre au [judo] en [septembre] » (10-20 fr): any
  hobby and any month make the same joke. Also « everybody has to play [hockey] » (10-16
  en) and « le grade de [colonel] » (10-16 fr).
- **The petty end left visible:** « he reported that the [men]'s room had been clean as a
  whistle » (10-07 en).
- **The drop handed over by its first half:** « i wanted to be a high [jumper] » (10-15
  en).
- **No frame:** « he often [bloated]… since the [reformation] » (09-30 en). Nothing points
  to the body, and any era fits.
- **Adjacent holes at the end:** « except possibly [folk] [dancing] » (10-19 en); « un
  [lexique] [rural] [autrichien] » (10-02 fr).

### 3. The small fact that proves a big thing

**What.** A small, everyday fact is in plain sight, and the hidden word is the big thing
it proves or turns into. Children who say « tu » to their fathers prove that democracy has
won. A neon sign for a brand of beer gives a face the majesty of a demon. This is the
build-up turned upside down: the small side is visible and the big side is hidden.

**Why it plays.** The player holds a fact that means nothing yet and asks what it proves.
The answer is out of scale, big where the fact is small. When it lands, the small fact
suddenly means something and the line flips. This is the kind of the day the user called
"perfect".

**Spot it.** Ask: does a small, concrete fact in the line mean nothing until one big word
explains it?

The cues:
- a stance with a strong adjective, followed by a small act (« de fougueux ennemis de… qui
  se faisaient… »)
- « c'est pour ça que »
- a grand noun set next to a brand, an object or a household habit

The bank also holds lines in reverse order, petty first and grand last, and they belong
here.

**Holes and punch.**
- Hide the big meaning and keep the small fact visible.
- The step from the fact to the meaning must be a thought, not a cliché association. Going
  from « sud-américain » to « [cocaïne] » is a cliché, so finding the word changes nothing.
- Never hide the fact itself when nothing else leads to it. The first 10-14 fr trio hid
  « tutoyer », the fact everything rests on, and that version of the day was impossible.
- The other two holes are words players say, with paths, taken from outside the line's
  fixed phrases.

**Start words.** Take the start from the big meaning's field, in the far half of the band:
« démagogie » for « démocratie », « pères » for « enfants », « gnome » for « démon ». Never
right next door to the answer.

**Examples.**
- **2026-10-14 fr, Tocqueville (liked, "perfect"):** « j’ai vu de fougueux [ennemis] de la
  [démocratie] qui se faisaient tutoyer par leurs [enfants]. »
- **2026-08-01 fr, Dazai (liked for « démon »; « lueur » is handed, and « bière » is one of
  a wide kind whose start « tisane » names a smaller family, *The narrowing ladder*):**
  « à la [lueur] rouge, vacillante, d'une réclame au néon pour une marque de [bière], le
  visage de horiki me semblait avoir pris la majesté d'un [démon] policier »

**Broken.**
- **A cliché step, with the joke readable blank:** « il prenait tant de [cocaïne] qu'il
  avait [hérité] d'un prénom sud-américain » (09-10 fr).
- **The fact hidden:** the first 10-14 fr trio, with « [tutoyer] ». Any verb for what
  children do to their parents could fill it.

### 4. The reasoning pushed to its end

**What.** The line states a cause, a count or a rule, and the hidden word is where that
logic lands, out of all proportion. The prose is so bad that one coherent paragraph would
earn a cake. A dog saved forty-one people and was killed by the forty-second.

**Why it plays.** The player does the reasoning, and the reasoning is the joke. The premise
is visible, so there is always a path. The conclusion is out of scale, so it still
surprises. Finding the word is the moment the logic snaps shut.

**Spot it.** Ask: is the conclusion far out of proportion to its premise? And is it more
than a stock exaggeration, like « tomber à la renverse tellement c'était beau »?

The cues: « tellement / si… que », « tant de… que », 'so… that', 'because', « c'est pour ça
que », or a number or count inside the line (« quarante et une… la quarante-deuxième »,
« six mois après », 'eighty percent'). About a quarter of all lines hold some logic, so
these cues pick nothing out on their own. What decides is which side of the logic is
hidden.

**Holes and punch.**
- Hide the word the logic ends on, and keep the premise visible.
- A premise word may be hidden when it has its own path and the conclusion is still hidden
  (Graeber's « [verbes] »).
- The logic must not write the word in: « je ne vais quand même pas [partir] sans lui » is
  a set phrase.
- The other two holes come from outside the logic's fixed phrases (never « arrêt
  [cardiaque] »), and at most one of them is handed. Days of this kind often handed over a
  frame word: « accouchaient », « sauva », « waited ».

**Start words.** Take the start from the conclusion's field, in the far half of the band:
« crucifié » for « tué », « dense » for « cohérent ». A start right next to the answer
finishes the logic for the player: « overdosed » at 104 for « died », « messire » at 102
for « dieu ».

**Examples.**
- **2026-09-08 fr, Graeber (liked; « gâteau » is one of a wide kind whose start « cookie »
  names a smaller family, *The narrowing ladder*):** « il s’appliquait à éviter tout recours
  aux [verbes]. sa prose était tellement nulle que j’avais décidé de m’offrir un petit
  [gâteau] si je tombais sur un seul paragraphe [cohérent]. »
- **2026-09-30 fr (not judged; « sauva » is handed):** « la statue du [saint-bernard] qui
  [sauva] quarante et une personnes et fut [tué] par la quarante-deuxième se dresse à
  l’entrée. »
- **2026-10-11 en, Davis (not judged; the logic is clean, but « postcard » could be any
  letter and the start for « died » sits too near):** « once, i [waited] so long to answer a
  [postcard] that my friend [died]. »

**Broken.**
- **The result of the logic left visible:** « soixante-quinze mille heures » is in plain
  sight (08-20 fr).
- **A stock exaggeration:** « vous tombiez à la renverse tellement c'était [beau] » (09-24
  fr).
- **Logic so tight it hands over the word:** « j'attends mon [cancer] : je ne vais quand
  même pas [partir] sans lui » (10-12 fr).

### 5. The riddle: a thing named by what it does

**What.** The line describes something by its habits and qualities, then names it: it never
sleeps or rests, it stretches without tearing, it works with no sense anyone can see. The
name is hidden and the qualities are visible. The thing comes from another world than the
subject: a machine for a lover, the wind for a landlady's nature, skin treated as a
material tested to its limit.

**Why it plays.** The line asks a small riddle and answers it. The visible qualities are
the clues, and the reasoning is direct: what never sleeps? A machine. The ranks confirm the
family, and when the word lands, the comparison finally makes sense.

**Spot it.** Ask: can you rewrite the line as a riddle whose answer is the hidden noun, and
does that answer come from another world than the subject? If it comes from the subject's
own stock (cold → wind, falling flesh → leprosy), it is a stock comparison, and the line
names the word for the player.

The cues: a definition shape ('X, n.', 'the one that…', 'by which is meant', « c'était ma
conception du »); « comme un », « tel un », « à la façon du », « je ne suis qu'un… ». Take
these only when the qualities are spelled out beside them. « comme un » or 'like a' alone
flags hundreds of stock comparisons.

**Holes and punch.**
- Hide the thing named. It is the punch.
- Keep at least one visible quality that, on its own, points to the thing's family. Hiding
  a quality is fine when a visible one gives its meaning (« j'dors pas » gives
  « [repos] »).
- Never hide a quality and the thing when each can only be reached through the other.
- Put the other holes in the plain part of the line, as words players say.

**Start words.** Use another thing from the same family, never a word for the visible
quality: « logiciel » for « robot », « musculature » for « peau », « gecko » for
« serpent ». The qualities already lead, so take the start from the far half of the band.
« seals » at 57 for « penguins » was too near.

**Examples.**
- **2026-09-01 fr, Rounhaa (liked):** « j'dors pas, j'prends jamais d'[repos] j'vais
  t'aimer et [mourir] comme un putain de [robot] »
- **2026-09-12 fr, Brillat-Savarin (liked):** « mirabeau disait d'un homme excessivement
  [gros], que dieu ne l'avait créé que pour [montrer] jusqu'à quel point la [peau] humaine
  pouvait s'étendre sans rompre. »
- **2026-08-18 fr, Kafka (not judged; every hole has a path):** « cette hôtelière avait une
  nature [intrigante] qui travaillait à la façon du [vent], sans aucun sens apparent,
  d'après les [ordres] lointains sur lesquels on n'avait jamais droit de regard »

**Broken.**
- **A stock comparison:** « comme la [lèpre] fait tomber la chair qui recouvre les os »
  (08-02 fr); « comme un [vent] de l'est, tu m'as refroidi les [os] » (08-26 fr).
- **Both sides hidden:** « aussi [involontaire] que la [transpiration] » (10-17 fr).
  Neither side had a visible anchor, and it took 36 guesses.
- **A quality too wide:** « compact comme une [pile] » (09-29 fr).
- **No qualities given:** « jeté l'[allumette] dans le dos du [cygne] » (08-27 fr). Nothing
  leads to a swan.
- **A chain where each hidden word calls the next:** « une paire de [lunettes] et, tel un
  dieu [myope], scrute la [terre] » (10-13 fr).

### 6. The cold exact word

**What.** The line talks about people, love, a body or a scene in the vocabulary of
science, administration or zoology: « mammifères intelligents, qui auraient pu s'aimer »,
or a « presentation » of a bare bottom. One hidden word is that cold, exact word. The bank
also holds the reverse (warm human words laid on stars or animals), which no day has used
yet.

**Why it plays.** The line names its subject plainly, so the player reaches the meaning
(beings, people, animals), and the ranks lead from the common word to the exact one:
« primates » at rank 2 and « animaux » at rank 9 lead to « mammifères ». The landing is a
change of voice: the line turns cold, funny and sad at once. In this kind the hard step is
the word itself, so a trio carries one hard hole and no more.

**Spot it.** Look for technical or learned nouns (species and families, chemistry,
statistics, administrative or theory words) inside a sentence about love, bodies, a couple
or an animal scene, with the subject seen from too far away. Then ask: does the line say
plainly what the cold word refers to?

**Holes and punch.**
- Hide ONE cold word, the punch, and only when the visible words plainly name what it
  refers to: two people who could have loved each other are beings.
- Choose the most common word of its field (« mammifères », not « mustélidés »). A rare
  stand-in next to a common word that means the same gets buried.
- The other two holes are words players say, each with a path. A second exact word passes
  only when the common word players type sits right beside it: on 09-05, « lumière » is
  rank 1 for « luminosité ».
- Never hide a cold word inside a fixed phrase that players either know or don't (« une
  [construction] idéologique »).

**Start words.** Take the start from the same cold field, on the word's direct meaning:
« néandertaliens » for « mammifères », « staging » for « presentation ». Never a rarer
member of the field, and never a start whose first idea leaves the field: « beauté » for
« luminosité » reads as a riddle.

**Examples.**
- **2026-09-05 fr, Houellebecq (liked):** « [mammifères] intelligents, qui auraient pu
  s'aimer, ils se [contemplaient] dans la grande [luminosité] de ce matin d'automne. »
- **2026-10-14 en (a good line once « knickers » was swapped out):** « i once read that
  [presentation] of one's backside might prove [useful] if one were being attacked by an
  adult male [chimpanzee]: "pull down your knickers and show him your bare bottom!" »

**Broken.**
- **Buried:** « dissociating love from [propagation] » (10-18 en), where players say
  reproduction; « un [charnier] datant du moyen âge » (10-08 fr); « la grâce des
  [mustélidés] » (09-28 fr).
- **No step to the cold word:** « whose capacity for [suffering] verged on [rapacity] »
  (10-01 en).
- **A fixed phrase:** « une [construction] idéologique » (08-07 fr), where players said
  fiction and invention.
- **Two hard words in one trio:** 09-16, 09-22, 09-23 and 10-05 fr. On these days, the
  median share of players who finished was 40%.

### 7. The bent phrase

**What.** The line calls up a phrase everyone knows (an idiom, a saying, a convention, a
sound pattern) and bends it. The hidden word is where it bends: « tu l'appelles mère
patrie, je l'appelle dame [nation] », heard as damnation.

**Why it plays.** The visible half calls up the known phrase, so the player reaches for its
usual ending. The meaning of the line steers them to the bent one, and the ranks confirm
it. The landing is a pun going off, or a sound pattern closing (clic-clac, flik flak…
« [zigzag] »).

**Spot it.** It turns up mostly in songs and rap, and also in moralists who twist a famous
saying. In the bank, most of the song lines are this kind. Ask: does the visible half call
up a phrase every player knows, and does the hidden word bend that phrase rather than
complete it?

**Holes and punch.**
- Hide the bent word, and keep visible the part that calls up the known phrase.
- Never hide the word that the known phrase writes in unbent (« partir sans lui »). That is
  a mirror.
- Sound confirms the meaning and never replaces it: at least two visible words must show
  the pattern.

**Start words.** Base the start on what the bent word means in the line, not on the
phrase: « faction » for « nation », « decomposing » for « dead ».

**Examples.**
- **2026-09-06 fr, Alpha Wann (liked; it is also a pair):** « dis à la france que tout se
  paie, ce pays est en [stagnation]. ici, c'est racisme et vente d'armes, des [clodos] à
  chaque station, tu l'appelles mère patrie, je l'appelle dame [nation] »
- **2026-10-17 en, Mac Miller (not judged; every hole has a path, and the dead faces are
  the dead presidents on banknotes):** « i went [pro], made [profit], now i keep some
  [dead] faces in my pocket »

**Broken.**
- **The phrase writes the word:** « je ne vais quand même pas [partir] sans lui » (10-12
  fr).
- **Half bent:** « compact comme une [pile] et souriant comme une [porte] » (09-29 fr).
  « porte » lands through « aimable comme une porte de prison », but « pile » could be any
  compact object.
- **Sound without meaning:** « capacity… verged on [rapacity] » (10-01 en).

This kind has the thinnest evidence of the seven: one liked day uses it in part, and the
rest are not judged. It matters most now that a song day shows its whole stanza.

## Lines that fit no kind

About 12% of the bank fits no kind (16% in French, 9% in English). Most of these lines are
one of three things:
- scene mechanics: 9 of Manchette's 15 lines
- abstract essay lines: 7 of Barthes's 14, and Debord
- *Le Joueur*: 5 of its 9 lines

Such a line can still make a good day, but rarely. Three of the 20 liked days fit no kind:
- **08-03 fr, de Vigan:** a city at nightfall and the asphalt moaning, an image carrying a
  thought. One word is handed (« ville »), one is an adjective nothing picks
  (« étrange »), and one is a medium search (« violence »).
- **09-15 fr, Houellebecq:** a deadpan exit. Two holes are scene mechanics chosen among
  siblings (« bureau », « retourne »), and only the start and the ranks narrow the punch,
  « psychiatre ».
- **09-09 fr, Machado:** the end of the world, and a watch. It has the build-up shape, but
  the petty end is visible and every hole is led straight to (89% finished, median 10
  tries). It was loved for the line read whole.

They worked because the line was very strong, and nothing in them is a recipe: the rules
above refuse some of their words (« bureau » and « retourne » are lotteries; « poche » is
written in by « dans sa ___ une montre »). Take a line with no kind only when, read aloud,
it beats the built candidates side by side. Expect such a day to be easy, or to rest on its
start words.

## The three words: the main word, then two against it

The method, in the words of the curator who chose the loved days:

> once I find a good line, there's always a word I'm sure I want to pick, then I chose 2
> other words that works well with my first pick, not too close, not too easy, not too
> hard, has a good starting word, balance the sentence well, is fun to find, etc.

The word you are sure of is the MAIN word: the punch its kind names, or the word a line
with no kind lands on. It is named once and never swapped: a fact against it sets the line
aside, and a fact against one of the two others means choosing another word against the
same main word. Then two words AGAINST it, by the rules above and its kind's *Holes and
punch*:

- **Not too close.** One idea per hole; never the main word's own idea; never the partner
  of a visible word (*No mirror*); never two words found only through each other (*No
  locked pair*); never right beside another hole (« [folk] [dancing] »; « [éleveur] de
  [pigeons] » has a word between).
- **Not too easy.** At most one word the line writes in, and never the main word; never a
  word the words right around it write in (*Per trio*).
- **Not too hard.** Words players say, at most one unsaid word in the trio; never a
  lottery (*The narrowing ladder*) and never a word the line gives no clue to.
- **A good start word.** A start in its band that opens its path (*The start words*).
- **Balance the line.** The three spread over the line's parts; none hides the words the
  others' paths run through, and one found word helps find the next.
- **Fun to find.** The image: « lueur », « bière », « cafard » — when it lands, you see.
  The exact word, clinical, technical or learned where a reader would put a vague one:
  « mammifères », « luminosité », « stagnation » — found, it gives the line its coldness.
  The register a notch off: « clodos » where prose would say *sans-abri* — the frame lets
  you reason toward the meaning, the word shifts the voice.

Ask: when this word is revealed, does the line mean more than it did?

## The start words

The start word is where the play begins: the first clue, and the path of guesses it opens.
Players think by ASSOCIATION. Each hole's start comes from its band, the words ranked 100
to 200 from the hidden word on its map; the far half (around 150–200) gives less than the
near edge.

**The main word's start comes first, by its kind**, as each kind's *Start words* says. For
a word of a wide kind, its FIRST idea names the ladder's smaller family, never the whole
kind: « papillons » for « pigeons », « cookie » for « gâteau », never « paintball » for
« judo ».

**Then each of the two others' starts, so the day is neither handed over nor out of
reach**: the main word's start in view, the harder the rest of the day, the more a start
may give.

A start is never TOO FAR from the word, even when the line makes it obvious: once the word
is found, the player must never feel the start made no sense. The link is in the start's
EVERYDAY sense — the one most players read first: « riffs » for « jokes » is comedy only to
those who know the stand-up meaning, and most read music.

Next to the meaning, never another name for it: « marriage » or « heartbreak » for
« divorce », not « nazi » for « facho » nor « blue » for « sky » — a synonym leaves the
player nothing to find. Never a member of the kind the word itself names: players name the
kind, and that is the word (« Shiva » for « Dieu »: they typed « dieu » at once; « parme »
for « rose »: a search says "a pink").

A start gives the secret away when the line leaves only one or two guesses between them:
- « frères » ← « neveux »: with the mother already in the line, family, then brothers —
  one or two tries.
- « chambre » ← « tanière »: a word for a teenager's room.
- « parfaitement » ← « admirablement »: a synonym in costume.
- « chaussettes » ← « baskets »: shoes, then socks — two automatic steps, no thought
  (2026-10-11, testers: "absolutely no fun"); « moufles » at 106 did the same.
- « bassine » ← « amphore », in « mis à tremper… dans une ___ de matière plastique »: the
  line calls the word whatever the start; a hole like that needs the rest of the day to
  carry the search.

### One idea, the start's first one

A start carries ONE idea its hidden word shares, and that idea is the FIRST one the start
brings where it stands — the user's own reading: « fracas » carries the idea of noise, and
« rugissements » is noise: good. « poids » carries a physical measure, and « place » is
one: good. « berceau » for « cercueil » carries a body in something, which is very vague,
and it carries far stronger ideas — a baby, a birth, rocking — that lead nowhere near the
word: the user's round made no progress at all. « cadavre » would have been the start: the
dead body the coffin holds, its first idea.

The shared idea may be a whole family, as wide as noise: the good starts above share theirs —
« tisane » for « bière » a drink, « papillons » for « pigeons » creatures people collect,
« cartier » for « rolex » a luxury brand. Wide is not vague: the start's first idea IS the
family. A start whose shared idea is not its first one is a RIDDLE: it makes sense only
once the word is found (« berceau » reads as « cercueil » only through « du berceau au
cercueil »).

## Difficulty is part of taste

Difficulty belongs to the HOLE, not to the word: the word, the line around it and its
start word together. The same word can be free in one line and out of reach in another,
and a start can open the path or close it.

Fun dies at both ends, and real rounds show it:
- **Too easy.** 2026-09-21: « l'[enseignant] de la classe voisine accourut… le [silence]
  se faisait instantanément » — the line calls the words, and a third of the players
  typed a secret as their first guess (median 5 tries). 2026-09-24: three ordinary words
  with starts sitting right beside them (« nulle » ← « terne », « beau » ←
  « harmonieux »): median 7.
- **Too hard.** 2026-09-16: « même le [facho] me manquait », from « catho » — the line
  gives no reason to go there: 4% of the players found it within 30 tries. 2026-09-23:
  « elle aimait le [héros] (Janko) », from « capitaine »: 4%. 2026-09-13: « j'ai appris à
  [lâcher] d'emblée que… », from « avancer »: 6%.
- **The loved days** (the September ones; August had too few players to measure) were
  finished by 55% to 89% of their players, medians of 8 to 28 tries — the Machado day by
  89% (median 10), Graeber by 81% (median 19), Fabre by 70% (median 28), Houellebecq's
  « mammifères » by 55% (median 16). Fun comes first; the start words tune the rest.

The aim: never frustrating for the slow players, never handed to the fast ones. At least
70% of the players finish the day — below that, too many stop playing the game — and the
fast ones still search: 09-21, finished by 69% with a median of 5 tries, was dead. A day
is as hard as its hardest hole. Tune difficulty with the start word, for a word that has a
path: a start tunes how far the search runs, and never gives a path to a word the line
leaves without one (a lottery, no clue). Never trade a living word for a dead one: a great
word that plays too hard needs a better start, not a duller word.

**The word players don't say.** Players find the meaning first, then type the words
they would use for it. A word they would never say for that meaning — the learned or
rare one beside a common word that means the same — keeps them circling its neighbours,
and no start word changes that: on 2026-10-05, players reached « faussaire » and
« contrefacteur », a third of them never got to « faux-monnayeur », and 30% of the
players finished the day. ONE such word makes a hard day, and a loved day may hide one —
« moucheron », « mammifères », « stagnation », « cafard » are that word, and they are the
punch, the image or the exact word. TWO in one trio made the worst days (2026-09-16,
09-22, 09-23, 10-05: a median 40% of the players finished, against 57% with one and 71%
with none; no such day reached 70%). Keep at most one, and only when it is worth the
search. When two are hidden, one goes, never the main word, and of the others the one more
fun to find stays: « clodos » over « stagnation » — the register a notch off lands with a
grin, the abstract noun only completes the thought.

A rare NAME of the thing passes (« saint-bernard », « post-it »: there is no other word for
it); a rare stand-in among commoner words meaning the same is BURIED, and code refuses it
(« faux-monnayeur » beside « faussaire », « charnier » among « cimetière », « ossuaire »,
« fosse »): players reach the idea, circle the commoner neighbours and stall.

In English, the players come from every English-speaking country: a word, or a sense of a
word, that only a small part of English speakers use is a word most of them don't say,
however natural it is where it is used; a word most of them say is not. « knickers » in
« pull down your ___ » (2026-10-14) was too British: it works for only a small part of
English speakers.

Ask: when a player has the meaning, would they say this word — or the common one beside it?

**Every day is somebody's first day.** A newcomer who solves their first day comes back
more than twice as often (37% against 16%); two thirds failed theirs, and a third of
those who never came back left within five guesses, before they understood the game.
So the two words beside the one players don't say are words they say. A word the line hands
over only weakly is fine when players say it (Graeber, 2026-09-08: 81% finished). Easy
is never handed over: the line leads, the player still searches (2026-09-21, median 5,
was dead).

## How to judge

- **Compare, don't approve.** Side by side, the best candidate wins — not the first
  acceptable one.
- **Decline only a dead day** — slop, plot, or a line with no main word that has a path.
  Skipping a day beats shipping a dead one; a good day that is not a favourite is still
  worth shipping, and the best of the candidates wins.
- **Say it in one line:** what the player will feel when each word lands.
